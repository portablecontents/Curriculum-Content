10th Grade ELA | NYS Regents Argument Structure

Motif: Rhetoric & Propaganda

The Weaponization of Words in Animal Farm

After a revolution, the biggest threat to freedom is often not physical force, but the manipulation of the truth. George Orwell’s allegorical novel Animal Farm shows how a hopeful animal rebellion turns into a brutal dictatorship under the pig, Napoleon. While the pigs eventually use violence to keep their power, their first and most effective tool for controlling the other animals is language. Orwell uses the motif of rhetoric to demonstrate how the pigs take over the farm; through Squealer’s manipulation of logic, his rewriting of history, and his use of fear, the pigs successfully override the animals' own memories and create an unfair society.

First, the pigs establish their control by using confusing rhetoric to manipulate logic and excuse their own greed. Shortly after the rebellion, the animals notice the milk and apples are missing, only to discover the pigs have taken them. Squealer is sent to justify this unfairness. Squealer argues, "Milk and apples (this has been proved by Science, comrades) contain substances absolutely necessary to the well-being of a pig. We pigs are brainworkers" (Orwell 28). Here, Squealer uses fake science to confuse the less educated animals. By deliberately using the specific label "brainworkers," he creates an artificial class divide that completely goes against the core rule that "All animals are equal." This shows that the animals are not controlled by force at first, but by their inability to untangle Squealer's tricky logic. With their logical defenses broken down by this fake science, the animals are now perfectly vulnerable to the pigs' next weapon: the erasure of the past.

Building on this initial confusion, the pigs further their control by deliberately rewriting history to make the animals doubt their own memories. When Napoleon decides to trade with humans, the animals vaguely remember a rule against this. Squealer immediately gaslights them. He challenges them, asking, "Are you certain that this is not something that you have dreamed, comrades? Have you any record of such a resolution? Is it written down anywhere?" (Orwell 51). Because the working animals cannot read or write well, they cannot rely on written records. Squealer exploits this weakness, turning the phrase "written down anywhere" into a trap. By forcing the animals to doubt the validity of their own "dreams" and memories without paper proof, the pigs become the sole owners of the truth, allowing them to break the rules without a fight.

Critics might argue that physical violence, not rhetoric, is the main way the pigs control Animal Farm. After all, Napoleon raises nine fierce dogs to attack his enemies and silence anyone who disagrees with him through pure terror. However, this perspective ignores the fact that violence is only used as a backup plan; rhetoric is the shield that stops the animals from rebelling in the first place. Whenever the animals begin to question the pigs' authority, Squealer ends his speeches with a rhetorical question: "Surely, comrades, you do not want Jones back?" (Orwell 44). This question is not a logical argument, but an emotional threat. By constantly bringing up the specific fear of "Jones," Squealer paralyzes the animals' critical thinking. The dogs might enforce the rules, but it is the rhetoric of fear that makes the animals accept the rules voluntarily.

Ultimately, George Orwell’s novel serves as a stark warning about what happens when an uneducated public faces persuasive lies. The tragedy of Animal Farm is not just that the pigs become dictators, but that they do so using the language of equality. Through tricky logic, erasing history, and manipulating fear, Squealer’s rhetoric proves to be far more dangerous than Mr. Jones’s whip. The novel demonstrates that without the ability to critically analyze what leaders say, citizens are doomed to participate in their own oppression.
